# Case study draft — <project name>

Copy this file, rename it to the project slug (e.g. `dotlet.md`), and answer in
whatever state your writing is in. Fragments and half-sentences are fine — the
substance has to be yours, the polish is mine.

**Don't skip a question by writing something plausible.** Write "don't remember"
or "no number for this" instead. Anything invented becomes a thing you have to
defend in an interview.

---

## The facts (one line each)

- **Role:** what you were actually responsible for, not your job title
- **Timeline:** e.g. "Jan–Jun 2025, 6 months"
- **Team:** who else was in it — "2 engineers, 1 PM, me"
- **Skills used:** 4–6, pulled from what you really did

---

## 1. The situation

What existed before you got there? Who was using it, and what was going wrong
for them? Be specific about the people — "claims adjusters" beats "users".

>

## 2. Why it mattered

What was the cost of leaving it broken? Money, time, errors, churn, risk,
someone's afternoon. A number if you have one; a concrete story if you don't.

>

## 3. What you did

Walk through it roughly in order. Research, what you found, what you designed,
what you changed after feedback. Don't tidy it into a neat process diagram —
the detours are the interesting part.

>

## 4. The hardest decision

What was the real tradeoff? What did you give up, and why was that the right
call? This is the question that separates a portfolio from a slideshow.

>

## 5. What you tried that didn't work

An abandoned direction, a failed test, an assumption that turned out wrong.
Interviewers trust a case study more when it admits something.

>

## 6. The outcome

What changed after it shipped? Metrics if they exist. If it never shipped, say
so and describe what you learned — an honest "killed after the pilot" reads
better than a vague claim of success.
